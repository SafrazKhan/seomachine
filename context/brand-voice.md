# Doorware Brand Voice & Messaging

This document defines the Doorware brand voice, tone, and messaging framework. Reference this when writing all content to ensure consistency. Copy should sound like a senior door hardware specialist: precise, practical, and trustworthy. It should help both contractors and homeowners make the right decision quickly, without hype.

## Brand Voice Pillars

### 1. Technical first, sales second
- **What it means**: Lead with fit, function, compatibility, and durability. Style and finish support the story; they are not the main point.
- **How it sounds**: Specifications and application context up front; benefits tied to real installation and use cases.
- **Example**: "Fits 2-3/8\" or 2-3/4\" backset, supporting standard residential door prep."
- **Avoid**: Leading with aesthetics, vague quality claims, or style-heavy copy that obscures how the product actually works.

### 2. Confident and plainspoken
- **What it means**: Direct language and short sentences. You know the category; you do not need to shout.
- **How it sounds**: Clear, professional, and efficient—like a specialist who respects the reader’s time.
- **Example**: "Single-cylinder function provides keyed exterior access with interior thumbturn operation."
- **Avoid**: Fluff, exaggeration, vague superlatives, and marketing filler ("amazing," "unbeatable," "perfect for every home").

### 3. Accurate and grounded
- **What it means**: State only what is supported by source data. Credibility beats persuasion.
- **How it sounds**: Factual, cautious where data is thin; precise about standards, dimensions, and compatibility when provided.
- **Example**: "Solid brass construction for long-term durability in high-use entry applications."
- **Avoid**: Invented standards, certifications, dimensions, compatibility, or claims not present in product or spec data.

### 4. Helpful to trade and retail
- **What it means**: Explain why a detail matters so both tradespeople and homeowners can decide faster.
- **How it sounds**: Professional terminology made readable; short bridges from spec to "why it matters on the job or at home."
- **Example**: "2-3/8\" or 2-3/4\" backset fits common residential prep—match to your existing bore and strike."
- **Avoid**: Jargon with no context, or oversimplified copy that wastes a pro’s time.

## Tone Guidelines

### General tone: Precise, practical, trustworthy
Imagine an experienced door hardware specialist helping someone choose correctly the first time: clear on fit and function, calm under complexity, and never patronizing. You genuinely want the right product in the right opening—not the flashiest sentence.

### Tone variations by content type

**How-to / installation-oriented guides**: Patient, procedural, safety-aware where relevant.
- "Measure backset from the door edge to the center of the bore before you order."
- "Confirm thickness and hinge placement so the lock case clears the edge."
- "If prep doesn’t match, note the mismatch before suggesting a different function or trim."

**Strategy / selection advice**: Advisory, comparison-friendly, decision-oriented.
- "Start with function (passage, privacy, entry), then match backset and thickness to existing prep."
- "When fire or code context is provided, treat it as a hard constraint—not an upsell angle."

**Industry / category context**: Measured, factual; trends only when supportable.
- "Finish and style trends shift; backset and function requirements usually don’t."
- "Lead with compatibility and duty rating when the use case is commercial or high-traffic."

**Product and feature copy**: Technical-first, scannable, benefit-tied-to-spec.
- "Lead with what it is, where it’s used, then core fit details (backset, thickness, prep, function)."
- "Reinforce material and performance when data supports it—security, durability, corrosion or fire context if provided."
- "Close with concise selection guidance (function, finish, trim) when relevant—no spec dumps in prose."

## Messaging Framework

### Core brand messages

#### Message 1: Fit and function before flair
- **Concept**: The right hardware is the one that matches prep, function, and environment—not the one with the loudest adjectives.
- **Key Points**:
  - Lead with compatibility and application.
  - Tie benefits to measurable or stated attributes (material, function type, standards when provided).
  - Use finish and style to support choice, not to replace technical clarity.
- **Usage**: Product descriptions, category pages, comparison content, and any copy where a wrong spec wastes time and money.

#### Message 2: Trust through facts, not hype
- **Concept**: Authority comes from accuracy and restraint, not superlatives.
- **Key Points**:
  - Only claim what sources support.
  - Prefer concrete nouns and stated specs over empty quality words.
  - When uncertain, say less and stay factual.
- **Usage**: All public copy, especially SEO fields and merchandising text.

#### Message 3: Pros and homeowners deserve the same clarity
- **Concept**: One voice can serve trade and retail: precise for pros, plain enough for DIY—without talking down to either.
- **Key Points**:
  - Explain why a detail matters in one short phrase when helpful.
  - Keep terms professional but define by context when needed.
  - Avoid both vague DIY language and unexplained trade-only shorthand in customer-facing copy.
- **Usage**: Guides, FAQs, product detail fields, and support content.

#### Message 4: Readable first, SEO second
- **Concept**: Search terms belong in copy when they match intent and read naturally—never at the expense of clarity.
- **Key Points**:
  - Integrate high-intent terms naturally; avoid keyword stuffing.
  - Use SEO metadata fields for focus and length discipline, not for repeating spammy phrases in body copy.
  - Brand and SKU references stay factual and tied to real product data.
- **Usage**: `seo_title`, `seo_description`, keywords, and on-page copy for merchandising and content pipelines.

### Value propositions

**For contractors and installers**:
"Specs-first copy that speeds verification—backset, function, prep, and materials stated clearly so you can match real job conditions without decoding marketing copy."

**For homeowners and DIY buyers**:
"Plain language on what the product is for and what to check before you buy, so you order the right item the first time."

**For catalog, ecommerce, and AI-assisted merchandising**:
"Consistent, grounded product voice that scales: same rules for human and generated fields—facts in, accurate copy out."

## Writing style guidelines

### Sentence structure
- **Vary length**: Mix short, decisive lines with slightly longer sentences only when explaining fit or compatibility.
- **Active voice preferred**: "Fits standard residential prep" not "Standard residential prep can be accommodated."
- **Average length**: Aim for clarity; favor shorter sentences in merchandising and bullets.
- **Clarity first**: If a sentence needs two reads, split it or swap jargon for a concrete detail.

### Paragraph structure
- **Length**: Keep paragraphs compact—often 2–4 sentences in narrative product copy unless a guide requires more.
- **One idea per paragraph**: Product type and use, then fit, then performance, then selection tips—each in its own block when space allows.
- **Transitions**: Direct ("Next, confirm…" / "If your door is…"); avoid filler transitions.
- **White space**: Prefer short paragraphs and bullets for scans on category and PDP contexts.

### Word choice
- **Concrete**: Name backset, function, material, and application when data allows.
- **Specific**: "Solid brass" or "stated grade" when in source data—not "premium materials" with no referent.
- **Active verbs**: Fit, supports, provides, matches, installs (where accurate)—not "delivers an experience."
- **Avoid fluff**: Cut "very," "really," and "actually" unless they change meaning. Avoid vague claims ("fits most doors") without qualification.

### Terminology

**Say this** → **Not that**
- Backset, function (e.g., single-cylinder, passage), prep, strike → vague "fits standard doors" (without which standard)
- Stated material or finish from data → "premium" / "top quality" with no spec
- "High-use entry" or "residential prep" when true → "every home" or "any project"
- Compatibility stated in inches or product terms → "universal fit" unless truly universal per source
- Benefit tied to a fact ("keyed exterior, thumbturn interior") → "amazing convenience"

## Content formatting

### Headlines
- **Be specific**: Prefer "Entry lock: single-cylinder, adjustable backset" over "Great door hardware."
- **Include benefit or use case**: Say what the reader learns or gets (fit, function, application).
- **Use numbers and concrete nouns** when they aid scanability (sizes, counts, "complete guide" only when content delivers).
- **Keep SEO titles concise**: Target roughly 45–60 characters when possible (max ~70 where the channel allows).

### Subheadings
- **Mirror the decision path**: Function → fit → materials/performance → selection notes.
- **Front-load keywords** that match real queries, naturally.
- **Avoid empty labels**: Not "Overview" alone—"What it is and where it’s used" when that matches the content.

### Lists
- **Parallel structure** in feature bullets (same grammatical pattern).
- **Feature line pattern**: `Benefit — practical explanation` (em dash or hyphen consistent with your template).
- **No raw spec dumps** in narrative bullets; no document links inside narrative bullets unless the channel explicitly allows it.

### Calls-to-action
- **Action-oriented**: "Confirm backset and thickness," "Compare functions," "See compatible trim"—not vague "Learn more" unless destination is clear.
- **Honest**: Do not imply guarantees ("perfect fit") without support in data.
- **Next step matches intent**: Spec verification for buyers; deeper education for researchers.

## Voice examples

### Excellent Doorware voice

"Solid brass construction for long-term durability in high-use entry applications. Fits 2-3/8\" or 2-3/4\" backset, supporting standard residential door prep. Single-cylinder function provides keyed exterior access with interior thumbturn operation—match function and backset to your existing bore before ordering."

**Why this works**:
- Opens with material and application, not adjectives.
- States fit in real measurements and common prep context.
- Names function clearly and ends with a practical selection cue.
- Every line is defensible from typical product data—no invented certs or vague superlatives.

### Not Doorware voice

"This amazing set is perfect for every home. Top-quality craftsmanship with unbeatable elegance. Fits most doors."

**Why this fails**:
- Hype ("amazing," "unbeatable") instead of specs.
- "Every home" and "most doors" are vague and often untrue.
- No function, backset, or application detail—useless for a pro and risky for a homeowner.

## Audience understanding

### Who we write for

**Primary audience**: Contractors, installers, and serious DIYers choosing hardware for real openings.
- Need fast verification of prep, function, and compatibility.
- Penalize wrong specs with callbacks, returns, and lost time.
- Respond to clear technical language and respect for craft.

**Secondary audiences**:
- **Homeowners**: Want confidence they are ordering the right thing; need plain explanations without condescension.
- **Purchasing / procurement**: Need SKU alignment, repeatability, and copy that matches spec sheets.
- **Ecommerce and merchandising stakeholders**: Need consistent, scalable rules for titles, bullets, and SEO fields.

### What they care about

**Top priorities**:
1. Will it fit my door and prep?
2. Is the function right for the room or entry (passage, privacy, keyed, etc.)?
3. Durability and security appropriate to use case.
4. Clear finish and style alignment when aesthetics matter.
5. Fast trust: accurate copy, no surprises on delivery.

**Pain points**:
1. Wrong backset or function ordered.
2. Marketing copy that hides incompatibility.
3. Missing or vague specs online.
4. Inconsistent terminology between channels.
5. Returns and rework from "universal" claims that are not universal.

### How to serve them

- **Respect time**: Lead with what matters for the decision—fit, function, environment.
- **Make it verifiable**: Tie claims to data shoppers can cross-check (SKU, stated dimensions, function names).
- **Explain the why briefly**: One clause on why backset or function matters beats a paragraph of brand story.
- **Acknowledge trade reality**: Callbacks and returns are expensive—accuracy is a courtesy.
- **Stay calm**: Confidence without shouting builds trust.

## Doorware pipeline reference (AI product copy)

Use this when generating or reviewing AI-authored merchandising fields so operational rules stay aligned with brand voice.

### Scope: AI-authored fields only

Applies to AI-generated text fields (for example: `text.description_html`, `text.features`, `text.seo_title_ai`, `text.seo_description_ai`, `text.seo_focus_keyword`). Do not rewrite, regenerate, or mutate deterministic fields such as `legacy_product_id`, `sku`, `parsed_specs`, `spec_sections`, or `resources`—those come from structured transforms, not LLM prose.

### SEO usage policy

- Use existing SEO inputs (e.g., `text.seo.title`, `text.seo.description`, `text.seo.keywords`) as guidance.
- Integrate high-intent terms naturally; readability first.
- Keep brand and SKU references factual; tie to real product fields (e.g., `product_data.core.sku` and vendor data).

**SEO output style (typical targets)**:
- 2–4 short paragraphs in `text.description_html` using HTML `<p>` tags only.
- 6–8 concise `text.features` bullets using `Benefit — practical explanation`.
- `text.seo_title_ai`: ~45–60 characters preferred (max ~70 where required).
- `text.seo_description_ai`: ~135–160 characters preferred (max ~170 where required).
- `text.seo_focus_keyword`: one exact keyword chosen from the provided SEO keyword list—not invented.

### Product page writing pattern

1. Start with what it is and where it is used.
2. Add core functional fit (backset, thickness, prep compatibility, function type).
3. Reinforce material/performance when data supports it (security, durability, fire/corrosion context if provided).
4. End with concise selection guidance (function, finish, trim) when relevant.

**Do not**: Dump raw specs as prose, repeat full spec tables in narrative, or embed document links inside narrative bullets unless the channel explicitly requires it.

### Provenance and schema (ai_content)

Maintain separation between generated copy and source inputs—for example, `text` (AI-authored) vs `text_raw` (provenance from upstream JSON). Primary narrative rewrite source should remain the canonical product description input; focus keywords must be selected from provided keyword inputs, not invented. Do not rewrite deterministic id/spec/resource fields in LLM output.

### Agent checklist before final LLM output

- [ ] No fabricated claims, standards, or dimensions.
- [ ] No links in `text.description_html` or `text.features` unless the workflow explicitly allows them.
- [ ] No mutation of specs, resources, or id/sku fields in LLM output.
- [ ] Tone is practical, professional, and non-hype.
- [ ] If uncertain, reduce claims and stay factual.

## Quality checklist

Before publishing any content, verify:

- [ ] **Voice**: Sounds like Doorware—technical-first, plainspoken, accurate, helpful to trade and retail.
- [ ] **Tone**: Appropriate for content type (guide vs product vs news) and audience.
- [ ] **Value**: Helps the reader verify fit, function, or next steps—not just describe the brand.
- [ ] **Clarity**: Complex prep or compatibility explained simply where space allows.
- [ ] **Accuracy**: Technical details match source data; no invented certifications or measurements.
- [ ] **Examples**: Real measurements, function names, and use cases when relevant.
- [ ] **Action**: Clear verification or selection steps where appropriate.
- [ ] **Messaging**: Aligns with fit-first, fact-first, and readability-first principles.
- [ ] **Terminology**: Preferred hardware terms; no vague universal claims without support.
- [ ] **Empowerment**: Reader can make a confident, informed choice.
